## The one-sentence stance

You discovered your argument in one order; the audience must *accept* it in a different one. **Order by
the questions the audience asks in the order they ask them — never by the order you found the answers.**
A research paper can afford chronological discovery because the reader controls the pace and can
re-read; a talk cannot, because the listener has one pass at your speed. So the ordering job is not
"present my findings" — it is **sequence belief-shifts so each one is reachable from the belief the
audience already holds, ending on the single decision the talk exists to move.** Every framework below
is a tested pattern for doing exactly that; each has a default and a flip, and you pick by reading the
room's starting beliefs, not by taste.

<a name="conclusion-first"></a>

## Conclusion-first: the default, and the three conditions that flip it

**Default: lead with the conclusion.** State your answer first, then support it. This is the
consultant/Minto/BLUF discipline, and the reasons are durable: the audience can hold one pass only, so
giving them the destination first lets them slot every later point into a frame instead of holding
fragments in suspense; it respects an answer-hungry audience's time; and it makes your own argument
self-auditing, because if you cannot state the conclusion in one sentence you do not yet have one.

> **Durability note.** "Conclusion-first is *proven* to raise comprehension/retention" is **overstated**
> — it is a well-justified practitioner heuristic with partial, indirect support (advance organizers,
> which are mixed and prior-knowledge-dependent), not a cognitive law. The serial-position studies often
> cited were word-list recall, not argument comprehension, and persuasion order (primacy vs recency) is
> genuinely contested. Treat answer-first as an evidence-informed **default**, not a law. (Full ledger:
> `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.)

**The three conditions that flip it to question-first / suspense-first** — when leading with the answer
would get it *rejected before it is understood*, or would *waste the payload*:

| Flip condition | Why conclusion-first fails here | What to lead with instead |
|---|---|---|
| **The conclusion will be rejected cold** (it contradicts a belief the audience holds firmly, or you have no ethos yet) | Stated first, it bounces off as "obviously wrong" and they stop listening | Lead with the *shared premise* and the *question*; earn the conclusion step by step so it arrives as the only place the logic could land |
| **The value of the talk IS the surprise** (a reveal, a demo, a "you won't believe what we found") | Spoiling the punchline spends the one asset the talk has | Build the gap; withhold the answer to land the contrast (bounded to the reveal beat — see `context-playbooks.md`, demo) |
| **The audience must first *feel the problem*** (they don't yet believe a problem exists, e.g. a visionary/keynote room) | An answer to a question they aren't asking lands as irrelevant | Open the gap — make them want the answer — *then* deliver it |

Note the asymmetry the flips encode: **the more answer-hungry and trusting the room, the harder you
front-load the conclusion; the more skeptical or unmotivated the room, the more you must earn it before
stating it.** The default is conclusion-first because most high-stakes rooms (execs, reviewers, busy
readers) are answer-hungry; the flips are real but bounded.

<a name="pyramid"></a>

## The Minto pyramid: grouped, ordered, MECE — with the attribution kept honest

The **Minto pyramid** is the canonical structure for conclusion-first argument: a single governing
claim at the top, supported by a small set of grouped sub-claims, each in turn supported by evidence —
a tree the listener descends only as far as they need.

```
                 GOVERNING CLAIM (the one-sentence answer)
                /              |               \
      Supporting        Supporting         Supporting
       claim 1            claim 2            claim 3      ← MECE: no overlap, no gap
      /    \             /    \             /    \
  evidence evidence  evidence evidence  evidence evidence
```

Three rules make the tree hold:

1. **Each group is MECE** — Mutually Exclusive (the sub-claims don't overlap) and Collectively
   Exhaustive (together they cover the parent with no gap). A non-MECE group is the most common
   structural defect: either two points are the same point (overlap, so one is filler) or a point the
   conclusion depends on is missing (gap, so the argument has a hole the audience will find).
2. **Ideas in a group share one logical kind** — all reasons *why*, or all steps *how*, or all parts
   *of what* — never a mix, because a mixed group cannot be checked for MECE.
3. **Order within a group by importance to the audience's decision**, not by the order you produced
   them.

> **Attribution kept honest (do not overstate in a sophisticated room).**
> - **MECE** — Barbara **Minto** coined the *term* and its consulting application but **credits the
>   underlying logical concept to Aristotle** (her own words). She invented the term/application, not
>   the logic.
> - **The Pyramid Principle's publication date is genuinely contested** — "1985" is not cleanly the
>   first edition; cataloged editions and Minto's own site disagree (1978/1981/1985/1987/1996). **Pin
>   the specific edition (ISBN/publisher) before attributing any single year.**
>
> Both points, with the exact quotes and edition catalog, live in `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.

**Why the pyramid, not the journalist's inverted pyramid.** The two look alike (both put the headline
first) but rank their middles differently. The **inverted pyramid** (journalism) ranks *facts by
newsworthiness* — built for a skimmer who may stop at any line — and the romantic "Civil-War reporters
feared cut telegraph wires" origin is **folklore, not history** (summary leads were under 1% through the
1860s–80s; the form is a gradual, cost-driven later development — see the attribution notes in
`delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`). The **Minto pyramid** ranks *claims by logical support*, which
is what a persuasive argument needs. **Borrow the pyramid, not the inverted pyramid, for a talk that
must persuade.**

<a name="vertical-logic"></a>

## Vertical logic: the operational test for whether the order holds

A pyramid can be MECE and still not *persuade*, because MECE only checks the horizontal groups. The
vertical relationship — between each claim and the one below it — is what actually carries the
audience down the tree. The durable test:

> **Every claim, read top-down, must provoke exactly the question its children answer; and every set of
> children, read bottom-up, must answer exactly the question its parent provokes.**

This is the **vertical-logic test**, and it is the single most useful audit of an argument's order:

1. Read the governing claim aloud. Ask: **"what question does a listener now have?"** ("Why?" or
   "How?" or "Which?")
2. The next level down must be *the answer to that exact question* — no more, no less. If the children
   answer a question the parent did not raise, you have a non-sequitur the audience will feel as a jump.
3. Recurse to the leaves. If at any node the question raised and the question answered diverge, the
   order is broken *there* — and that is the slide where you will lose the room.

A talk that passes the vertical-logic test top-to-bottom is one where each point arrives as the answer
to the question the previous point just made the audience ask — which is the operational meaning of
"order by their next question." (This pairs with the **titles-only test** in
`slides-visuals-cognitive-load.md`: if the slide titles, read in sequence, reconstruct the whole
argument with no jump, the vertical logic holds at the deck level.)

<a name="staircase"></a>

## The audience-question staircase: order by their next question, not your outline

The pyramid tells you the *structure*; the staircase tells you the *sequence in time*. Build the talk's
running order by writing down the questions the audience asks **in the order they ask them**, starting
from the belief they walk in with and ending at the decision you want — then answer each in turn. Each
answered question raises the next; the talk is the staircase of those questions.

**The template — fill the right column from your audience's actual starting beliefs:**

| # | The audience's question at this step | What the talk answers here (your content) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What is this even about / why am I here?" | The stake / the gap — what's at risk or possible (the Complication) |
| 2 | "What are you claiming?" *(or, on a flip: held to step 6)* | The governing sentence — your one-sentence answer |
| 3 | "Why should I believe *that's* the right framing of the problem?" | The problem stated so your answer is the natural one |
| 4 | "Why is this hard / why hasn't it been solved?" | The delta vs. prior work; what makes it non-trivial |
| 5 | "What exactly is your idea?" | The ONE key idea, concretely (narrow and deep, not broad) |
| 6 | "Why does it work?" | The mechanism / the core evidence for the claim |
| 7 | "How do I know it really works / where's the proof?" | Validation, results, the decision-relevant numbers |
| 8 | "What are the catches?" | Limits and threats — pre-stated, never buried (builds ethos) |
| 9 | "So what do you want me to do?" | The single explicit ask / next action |

The staircase is **the same nine questions in roughly this order across most persuasive talks**; what
changes per context is *which steps compress to a sentence and which expand to minutes* (an exec
collapses 1 and 3; a keynote expands 1; a defense expands 6–8). Crucially, the order is the
*audience's* — if your outline answers question 5 before question 3, you are explaining your idea before
the room agrees there's a problem it solves, and they will not be listening. Re-order until each row's
question is one the previous row just made them ask. (Per-context expansions/collapses: `context-playbooks.md`.)

<a name="scqa-scr"></a>

## SCQA, SCR, and BLUF: the introduction frameworks and when each fits

The opening of a talk has one job: move the audience from their starting belief to *wanting your
answer*, in the fewest words. Three named frames do this, and they are a **default plus two
escape hatches**, not a menu to pick from at random.

**Default — SCQA** (Minto's introduction structure): **Situation → Complication → Question → Answer.**

- **Situation** — the stable context the audience already accepts (the shared premise; no controversy).
- **Complication** — what changed or what's wrong: the tension that makes the status quo untenable.
- **Question** — the question the Complication forces (often left implicit once the tension is sharp).
- **Answer** — your governing sentence.

SCQA is the default because it *earns* the answer: it starts on common ground, builds the tension, and
lets the audience feel the question before you answer it — so the answer lands as resolution, not
assertion. It is the introduction form of the conclusion-first default with the on-ramp built in.

**Escape hatch 1 — SCR** (drop the explicit **Q**): **Situation → Complication → Resolution.** Use it
for an **answer-hungry, already-oriented audience** (the classic exec readout): they already feel the
problem, so the explicit Question wastes their time and a long Situation reads as condescension —
front-load the tension and get to the Resolution. **Minimize the Situation** to a single orienting
sentence and spend the airtime on the Resolution and decision-relevant numbers.

> **Durability note.** SCR (dropping the explicit Question) is **community lore, a useful compression —
> not official McKinsey doctrine**; and how much early attention you actually have, plus the
> often-cited "fixed attention span" figures, are **contested empirical claims** (peer-reviewed reviews
> find little support for a fixed 10–15-minute attention ceiling). Do not quote an X-minute attention
> figure as fact on stage. The durable, defensible principle is the one that survives without a number:
> **early attention is scarce and front-loaded — a long Situation for a room that already lives the
> problem burns that scarce budget and reads as condescension, so open on the tension.** (The contested
> figures and the studies that bound them: `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.)

**Escape hatch 2 — BLUF** (Bottom Line Up Front): collapse the on-ramp entirely and **state the
recommendation and the decision you're asking for in the very first sentence**, then support it. Use it
when the audience is maximally answer-hungry and the conclusion will *not* be rejected cold (e.g. a
trusted exec relationship, a status update). BLUF is SCR compressed to its limit: the Resolution comes
first and the Situation/Complication follow only as much as needed.

> **Attribution kept honest.** The **principle/phrase** "putting the main point at the beginning …
> (bottom line up front)" is codified in **U.S. Army Regulation AR 25-50**, but the **acronym "BLUF"
> appears nowhere in the regulation text** — it is military shorthand that grew up around it. "BLUF was
> formalized in AR 25-50" overstates it: the regulation formalizes the *principle*, not the acronym.
> (Details: `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.)

**Choosing among the three — by the room's answer-hunger and your standing ethos:**

| Audience starting state | Frame | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Needs to be brought to the problem; mixed prior; you must earn the claim | **SCQA** (default) | Build the question before the answer so it lands as resolution |
| Already feels the problem; answer-hungry; oriented | **SCR** | Drop the explicit Question; minimize Situation; lead the tension straight into the Resolution |
| Maximally answer-hungry; trusts you; conclusion won't be rejected cold | **BLUF** | Recommendation in sentence one; support follows |

The three are one spine with the on-ramp progressively shortened as answer-hunger rises — the same
logic as the conclusion-first flips above, applied to the opening.

<a name="world-shift"></a>

## Strategic narrative: the world-shift arc for a belief-change talk

When the goal is not "approve this decision" but **"change what the audience believes is true about the
world"** (a vision pitch, a category-defining keynote, a fundraise), the pyramid alone is too dry —
you need an arc that creates *forward pull*, not just logical descent. The durable pattern is the
**strategic-narrative / world-shift arc**, a four-step structure:

1. **Name a big shift in the world** — something undeniable that has *already changed* (a "why now"):
   the ground the audience stands on has moved, whether they've noticed or not. This is the
   Complication at the scale of an era, not a project.
2. **Show there will be winners and losers** — the shift creates stakes: those who adapt win, those who
   don't lose. This converts the abstract shift into personal consequence.
3. **Paint the promised land** — the specific, desirable future the *winners* reach. Make it concrete
   and pictureable, not a slogan; the audience must *want* to be there.
4. **Position your offering as the gift that gets them there** — your product / idea / framing is the
   tool the hero (the audience) uses to reach the promised land. **The audience is the hero; you are the
   guide**, never the protagonist.

The structural difference from the pyramid: the world-shift arc front-loads **pathos and a why-now**
before any claim about your offering, so the audience is *pulling* for the answer by the time you give
it. It is the conclusion-first "flip" (feel the problem first) executed at the scale of a worldview.

> **Durability note.** This is a **well-tested craft pattern, not an empirical law** — it is durable
> because it composes mechanisms that *are* better-evidenced (attention, concreteness, and narrative
> transportation, the absorption-into-story effect that measurably shifts beliefs and reduces
> counter-arguing). Do not justify it with the popular but contested "stories are 22× more memorable"
> claim — that figure is **apocryphal**; the real, verified memory effects are far smaller and
> task-dependent. The specific craft sources for this arc (the strategic-narrative writing and the
> investor "why-now" template that popularized it), the transportation evidence, and the 22× debunk
> with the real multipliers are all pinned with citations in `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md` and
> applied per-context in `context-playbooks.md` (investor pitch).

**Flip regime — shrink the register for skeptical experts.** The world-shift arc *dominates* for
visionary / pitch / keynote rooms. It **shrinks** for a skeptical expert audience (a research talk, a
defense, a technical review): there, over-large "world-shift" framing reads as hype and **costs**
ethos, so keep the why-now to a sober one-line motivation and let logos + ethos carry the room. Same
arc, dialed down — not abandoned, but registered for a room that punishes grandiosity. (Per-context
calibration: `context-playbooks.md`; the ethos/pathos/logos diagnosis: `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.)

## The Story Spine: a concrete scaffold for the narrative beats

When you need the world-shift arc (or any before/after story — a demo, a case study) as fill-in-the-
blank beats, the **Story Spine** is the most usable scaffold:

```
Once upon a time [the status quo the audience lives in]
…and every day [the routine pain of that status quo]
…until one day [the disruption — the shift, or your product entering]
…and because of that [a consequence]
…and because of that [the consequence cascades]
…until finally [the resolution — the promised land reached]
…and ever since [the new normal the audience now inhabits].
```

Map it onto the world-shift arc: *every day* is the pain the shift exposes; *until one day* is the
why-now; *because of that…* is winners/losers cascading; *until finally / ever since* is the promised
land and the new normal. The customer is the "hero" who moves through the beats; your offering is the
"until one day" turn, not the subject of the story.

> **Attribution kept honest.** The Story Spine is **Kenn Adams (1991)**, popularized via Pixar by
> **Emma Coats (2012)** — and is **distinct from Campbell's Hero's Journey (1949)**; don't conflate
> them. (Sources and the distinction: `delivery-objections-and-evidence.md`.)

**Use it as a scaffold, not a script.** The beats guarantee the arc has tension and resolution; they do
not excuse dressing a thin argument in story clothing. For a skeptical-expert room, collapse the
fairy-tale phrasing to its logical skeleton (status quo → tension → turn → consequences → resolution)
so the structure survives while the register stays sober — the same shrink-for-experts flip as the
world-shift arc above.
