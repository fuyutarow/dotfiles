## The one-sentence stance

Delivery is not performance polish bolted onto a finished deck; it is the **moment the cognition
change actually lands or fails to**, and the open/close/Q&A are the three highest-leverage windows.
The open buys you the right to be heard; the close fires the payload (the one decision); Q&A is
where the decision most often *actually moves*. None of these is improvised — each is designed
backward from the audience's initial state, exactly like the slides. And because persuasion fails
through one *binding constraint* at a time (credibility, feeling, or argument), the delivery job is
first **diagnosis** — find which of the three is the bottleneck — then spend your scarce attention
there, not uniformly.

## Open mechanics: the first ~2 minutes

Spend the first minute or two on **stakes, not throat-clearing** — you have only a short window before
the audience decides whether to keep paying attention. (No fixed "attention drops at N seconds" law is
well-evidenced — see the TED row in the myths table; this is a judgment heuristic about *where the
scarce opening budget goes*, not a measured limit.) The open does three jobs, in this order:

1. **Establish stakes / why-now.** Open the gap the talk will close: a world-shift, a concrete pain,
   a surprising number, a single vivid example. This is the "Situation→Complication" tension
   (`structure-and-narrative.md`). For an answer-hungry audience (exec), collapse it: state the
   recommendation in sentence one (BLUF).
2. **State, or visibly set up, the governing sentence.** The audience should know within ~2 minutes
   either *what you are claiming* (answer-first default) or *what question you are about to answer*
   (when the conclusion would be rejected cold — see the answer-first flip in
   `structure-and-narrative.md`).
3. **Earn the next two minutes.** Lead with your strongest material, not your throat-clearing.
   Cut the self-deprecating apology ("sorry, lots of slides", "I only had a week"), the
   agenda-recitation, and the personal-bio detour — all of them spend the scarce opening budget on
   things the audience did not come for.

**Default opener template (persuasive talk):**

> *"[Concrete stakes / why-now in one or two sentences]. The question this forces is [the audience's
> real question]. My answer — and what I'll show you today — is [governing sentence]."*

**Escape hatch.** For a reveal/demo whose value *is* the surprise, withhold the answer and build the
gap longer (see `context-playbooks.md`, demo). For a hostile/skeptical room, lead with ethos
(demonstrated competence + shared premise) before any claim, or the claim bounces off.

**Anti-pattern — the cold-open apology / agenda slide.** "Here's my outline; first some background…"
trains the audience that the front of your talk is skippable. Fix: open on the stake, not the map.

## Close mechanics: the explicit ask and the audience-as-hero

The single most common waste in the whole field is closing with **"Thank you. Any questions?"** — a
slide that asks for nothing. The close is the payload; design it as deliberately as the open.

1. **State the one next action, explicitly.** Not "I hope this was useful" but *"the decision I'm
   asking for today is X."* Name the single thing the audience should DO or BE (this is the goal
   from STEP 1; the close is where it gets cashed). The ask is bracketed — previewed early
   (BLUF) and restated at the end — so it never arrives as a surprise.
2. **Cast the audience as the hero, you as the guide.** You are not the protagonist who triumphs;
   you are the mentor who hands the hero the gift (the tool, framing, capital, decision) that
   enables *their* transformation. "Here is how *you* solve *your* problem," not "look what I built."
3. **Give them the carry-away.** End on the one sentence you want repeated in the hallway
   afterward — usually the governing sentence, sharpened.

**The ask's shape flips by context** (its *existence* does not):

| Context | The explicit ask |
|---|---|
| Research / conference talk | "Read the paper / adopt this framing / collaborate on the open question." |
| Investor pitch | "Invest / take the next meeting" — with the milestone the money buys. |
| Exec decision readout | "Approve option B" — the specific decision, with its cost and the cost of *not* deciding. |
| Thesis defense | (implicit) "Pass me" — earned via demonstrated command, not asked aloud. |
| Product / sales demo | "Start a trial / next meeting" — anchored to the buyer's stake. |
| Keynote / inspiration | The "action" softens to an *idea to carry and spread* — but still name it. |

**Escape hatch.** Pure-inspiration keynotes soften the transactional ask to an idea; the ask never
fully disappears, it changes currency.

## Objection-handling and Q&A as first-class design

Q&A is not the appendix to the talk — for many decisions (pitches, defenses, exec readouts) it is
**where the decision actually moves.** Design it as deliberately as the main line.

**Build the objection inventory first.** From the audience-initial-state model (`audience-and-message.md`,
STEP 2), every *doubt* axis becomes an FAQ entry. For each anticipated objection decide:

- **Pre-empt in the main line** — when the doubt is so load-bearing that an unaddressed audience
  will reject the whole talk (e.g., "but this doesn't scale", "your baseline is weak"). State it
  yourself and answer it; pre-stating a limitation *builds* ethos, it does not cost it.
- **Hold for Q&A with a backup slide** — when the doubt is real but narrower, or will only occur to
  a subset. Keep a **one-objection-per-backup-slide** arsenal *after* the close: the chart, the
  ablation, the cost breakdown, the security model. Pulling up the exact slide on demand is the
  single strongest credibility move available in Q&A.

**The decision-moves-in-Q&A consequence:** the main line's job is often to earn enough trust that
the room *engages*; the backup slides are the real argument. This is why a thesis defense or a
technical review correctly runs a sparse main line over a deep backup deck (the slide-density flip,
`slides-visuals-cognitive-load.md`).

**Q&A mechanics that protect the decision:**

- **Answer the question actually asked, then bridge.** A non-answer reads as evasion and *costs*
  ethos faster than a weak answer.
- **"I don't know — here's how I'd find out"** beats bluffing; in a credibility-sensitive room,
  bluffing detected once contaminates everything you said.
- **Pre-state limitations; never let them be discovered.** A limitation you raise is command; a
  limitation extracted from you under questioning is a wound.
- **Repeat / reframe the question** to convert a hostile framing into the question you can answer —
  honestly, not by dodging.

**Anti-pattern — contradiction-laundering.** Burying or omitting the obvious limitation so it can't
be raised. It always surfaces in Q&A, where it now looks like concealment. Fix: stage a backup
slide per anticipated doubt and pre-state the ones that matter.

## Binding-constraint diagnosis: ethos, pathos, logos

Persuasion needs all three — **logos** (the argument is sound and ordered), **ethos** (you are
credible: demonstrated competence + goodwill), **pathos** (the audience *feels* the gap). They do
not add linearly: a logically airtight, perfectly ordered talk still fails if ethos or pathos is the
binding constraint. So when a talk is not landing, **diagnose which one is binding** before adding
more of what you already have too much of.

| Symptom in the room | Likely binding constraint | The fix (add THIS, not more slides) |
|---|---|---|
| "Why should I believe *you*?" / arms crossed / "what's your data" | **Ethos** | Show demonstrated competence + goodwill: the result, the rigor, shared premise. Strip hype language. |
| Nodding along, then no action / "interesting, thanks" | **Pathos** | Make them *feel* the gap: concrete example, what-is vs what-could-be contrast, stakes. |
| Engaged but unconvinced / "I don't follow step 3" | **Logos** | Fix the staircase: the missing link, the MECE gap, the unsupported assertion. |

**Two oscillations are the engine of pathos+logos together** (apply *repeatedly* through the talk,
not once):

- **abstract ↔ concrete** — claim, then a sensory example, then the diagram, then generalize
  (concreteness: a claim the audience can picture is a claim it can hold).
- **what-is ↔ what-could-be** — present pain vs the possible future, alternating, to create forward
  pull (Duarte's contour).

**Flip regime.** The is/could-be contrast and emotional appeal *dominate* for visionary / sales /
keynote talks; they *shrink* for skeptical expert audiences (a defense, a technical review), where
over-emotional framing reads as a credibility red flag and **logos + ethos must carry the room.**
Manipulative open curiosity-gaps (clickbait) and grandiose language invert into a *trust* cost.
Always *close* a curiosity gap you open. And note: the popular delivery "mechanisms" (oxytocin,
neural coupling — see myths below) are *plausible-but-unproven causal levers*; lean on the safer,
better-evidenced grounds of **attention, concreteness, and narrative transportation**, not on a
neuro-mechanism you cannot defend if challenged.

## Time budget and rehearsal: compression as a discipline

Compression is not what you do after the talk is written; it is the discipline that *makes* it
clear. Treat the time cap as a forcing function.

- **Cut to fit; never shrink fonts.** The instant you reduce font size to fit more on a slide, the
  slide has too much on it — that is the signal to cut, not to zoom out. A **font floor** is valuable
  precisely *because* it forces the cut; the common default floor is **~30pt** (Kawasaki's 10/20/30
  rule — a compression heuristic, not a law: see `context-playbooks.md` §3). Treat the number as a
  forcing function, not a measured constant.
- **Rehearse against the clock, out loud.** Silent reading hides the slides where your own
  explanation has gaps. As a rule of thumb, a talk that overruns is not "too long" — the overrun
  *is* the fraction that doesn't carry the message; cut that, don't talk faster.
- **Budget by leverage, not by section size.** A useful default for a research talk is a *minority* of
  the time on motivation / why-this-matters and the *majority* on the one key idea ("there is no
  point 3") — narrow-and-deep beats wide-and-shallow. This is a heuristic split, not a measured ratio;
  do not spend equal time on every section just because it exists.
- **The titles-only pass IS the compression test.** If reading only your slide titles in sequence
  reconstructs the whole argument (`slides-visuals-cognitive-load.md`), the talk is already
  compressed; if it doesn't, no amount of trimming bullets will save it.

## Myths busted (with the accurate version)

**Never repeat any of these as true.** Citing folklore stats signals you didn't check sources and
hands a skeptical audience a free credibility hit. If you need the point, state the *accurate*
version or drop the number.

| Myth (do NOT repeat) | Accurate version |
|---|---|
| **"Stories are 22× more memorable than facts"** (attributed to Jennifer Aaker) | The 22× figure is **apocryphal** — no traceable study, not in Aaker's work; it traces to an uncited claim in Maxwell & Dickman (2007) misattributed to Bruner. Real, verified multipliers are far lower and task-dependent: **~2×** (Graesser et al. 1980) to **~6–7×** (Bower & Clark 1969). Stories *do* aid memory and reduce counter-arguing via **narrative transportation** (Green & Brock 2000) — say that, not 22×. |
| **"7-38-55 ⇒ 93% of communication is nonverbal"** (Mehrabian) | The 1967 studies measured only how listeners resolve **incongruent** cues when judging *feelings/attitudes* — single words, tiny all-female samples, facial photos. **Mehrabian himself disavowed** the generalization. There is no basis for "93% of a presentation is nonverbal." |
| **"People remember 10% of what they read … 90% of what they do"** / numbered **Dale's Cone of Experience** | Dale's 1946 Cone had **no percentages** and no retention research; the numbers trace to a **1967 unsourced Mobil Oil** training piece, repeatedly debunked (Subramony/Molenda/Thalheimer; Letrud & Hernes). Cite Mayer's multimedia principles and the **testing/retrieval effect** instead. |
| **"TED's 18 minutes is the brain's attention limit / prevents cognitive backlog"** | 18 min is an **administrative format choice** (Chris Anderson, for precision and discipline); the neuroscience rationale is retrofitted. Peer-reviewed reviews (Wilson & Korn 2007; Bradbury 2016) find little support for a fixed 10–15 min attention limit. Use the cap as a **compression discipline**, not a law. |
| **"PowerPoint caused the Columbia disaster"** | The CAIB report called endemic briefing-slide use an **illustration** of NASA's broken technical communication — a symptom in a chain dominated by the physical foam strike and organizational dysfunction — **not a named cause.** Tufte's stronger "the tool is defective" framing is his opinion, not the official finding. Valid lesson: bullet slides can hide reasoning — prefer assertion-evidence or a technical report for high-stakes engineering review. |
| **"Conclusion-first is *proven* to improve comprehension and retention"** | It is a well-justified **practitioner heuristic** (Minto, BLUF) with partial, *indirect* support (advance organizers — mixed, prior-knowledge-dependent; the levels effect; one news study). Serial-position/primacy studies were **word-list recall, not argument comprehension**, and persuasion order (primacy vs recency) is genuinely **contested**. Use answer-first as an evidence-informed *default*, not a cognitive law. |
| **"Stories work because they release oxytocin → empathy"** (Zak) / **"great speakers literally sync your brain to theirs"** | Zak's oxytocin mechanism rests on a **contested peripheral-blood proxy** in a literature whose flagship prosocial/trust effects often **fail to replicate**. Speaker-listener **neural coupling** (Hasson) is real but *correlates* with comprehension — **not a demonstrated persuasion lever.** Justify storytelling on attention, concreteness, and transportation. |
| **"A diagram is always worth a thousand words / visuals are always remembered better"** | Larkin & Simon (1987): informationally equivalent representations differ in **computational efficiency**; a diagram wins **only sometimes** (when the task exploits spatial locality/grouping). A task-irrelevant visual is **pure extraneous load**, not a free win. |

## The real evidence core to rely on

When you *do* want to ground a delivery or design choice, these are the load-bearing, citable
findings — use these instead of the folklore above.

- **Narrative transportation** — Green & Brock, *J. Pers. Soc. Psychol.* (2000). Absorption into a
  story measurably **shifts beliefs and reduces counter-arguing** — the defensible reason stories
  persuade (not "22× memory", not oxytocin).
- **Concreteness / picture-superiority** — concrete, picturable language is recalled and acted on
  better than abstraction. This is the evidentiary backbone of the abstract↔concrete oscillation and
  of "make the figure make an inference free."
- **Multimedia & redundancy effects** — Mayer's *Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning* and the
  redundancy/coherence/split-attention principles (Mayer & Moreno; Sweller). The validated harm is
  specifically **full verbatim on-screen text concurrent with a graphic under fixed pacing** — with
  documented **reversal conditions** (no competing graphic; key terms only; hard-to-decode audio;
  learner-controlled pacing). Details and the assertion-evidence study (Garner & Alley) live in
  `slides-visuals-cognitive-load.md`.
- **Advance organizers — true scope.** Ausubel's advance organizers help comprehension **mainly when
  prior knowledge is low and material is unfamiliar**; effects are mixed and prior-knowledge-
  dependent. This is *partial, indirect* support for answer-first — enough to justify it as a
  default, **not** enough to call conclusion-first a proven law.
- **Larkin & Simon (1987), "Why a Diagram is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words"** — the
  computational-efficiency account of when a figure beats prose. The honest charter for every figure
  decision: *what inference does this make free?*

## Attribution notes (who actually coined what)

Attribute precisely; the popular shorthands are wrong often enough to be a credibility risk in a
sophisticated room.

- **MECE** — Barbara **Minto** coined the *term* and its consulting application, but **credits the
  underlying concept to Aristotle** (her own words, McKinsey Alumni interview: *"it wasn't really I,
  actually, it was Aristotle, but I was the first one to abbreviate it and apply it to analyzing
  groups of ideas"*). She developed her methods during her McKinsey tenure (1963–1973), commonly
  dated "late 1960s." So: she invented the *term/application*, **not** the logical concept.
- **The Pyramid Principle — publication date is genuinely contested.** "1985" is **not** cleanly the
  first edition. A **1981** Minto International edition is cataloged (Internet Archive), referencing
  a **1978** original; Wikipedia dates *Logic in Writing and Thinking* to **1985**, but Minto's own
  site says it was **written in 1987**; the expanded *Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing,
  Thinking and Problem Solving* is **1996**. **Pin the specific edition (ISBN/publisher) before
  attributing any single year.**
- **BLUF** — AR 25-50 codifies the **phrase** "putting the main point at the beginning … (bottom
  line up front)" (present in the 2002, 2013, 2020 editions), but the **acronym "BLUF" appears
  nowhere** in the regulation text — it is military shorthand that grew up *around* it. So "BLUF was
  formalized in AR 25-50" overstates it: the regulation formalizes the *principle/phrase*, not the
  acronym.
- **Inverted pyramid (journalism)** — the "Civil War reporters feared cut telegraph wires" origin is
  **folklore, not history.** Content analysis (Errico et al. 1996/97) shows summary leads were **<1%
  through the 1860s–1880s**, rising sharply only 1890–1900; the form is a gradual post-Civil-War
  development driven by telegraph *cost*, wire services, and professionalization (Scanlan, Poynter
  2003; Mindich 1998). **Do not borrow the inverted pyramid for a persuasive talk anyway** — it ranks
  facts by newsworthiness (good for skimmers, weak for argument); the **Minto pyramid** ranks claims
  by logical support and is the one built for persuasion (`structure-and-narrative.md`).
- **SCR-over-SCQA** is **community lore, not McKinsey doctrine** — SCR (drop the explicit Question)
  is a useful compression for answer-hungry audiences, not an official prescription.
- **Story Spine** ("Once upon a time… every day… until one day… because of that… until finally…") is
  **Kenn Adams (1991)**, popularized via Pixar by **Emma Coats (2012)** — *distinct* from Campbell's
  1949 **Hero's Journey**. Don't conflate them.
- **"Sparkline"** was coined by **Tufte** for inline word-sized charts; Duarte only *repurposed* the
  word for presentation structure. Use "sparkline" for the chart, not the slide arc.
